Infrastructure maintenance, contact-based inspection, and emergency response
can benefit from aerial vehicles that act as a flying human hand with extreme
maneuverability, manipulation, and resiliency (MMR):maneuverabilityto fly in
arbitrary orientations to reach remote and tight locations;manipulationto point
sensors, turn valves, and press tools at arbitrary orientations;resiliencyto main-
tain accurate motion and force control despite disturbances from arbitrary direc-
tions, such as wind, ground effects, and friction. Realizing MMR on aerial vehi-
cles requires not only omnidirectional flight; it also requires (I) vectoring of max-
imum thrust in any direction, to maximize capacity for contact-force application
and disturbance rejection, (II) global stability, to enable control over any orienta-
tion/position, and (III) compact, standard designs that build upon platforms such
as quadrotors to inherit technological know-how. No current aerial vehicle simul-
taneously enables I–III, due to structural and control limitations that constrain
actuation. We present MorphQuad: a morphable quadrotor that enjoys MMR.
Key to our approach is a hardware and control co-design: on hardware, we inde-

Introduction
Infrastructure maintenance (1, 2), contact-based inspection (3, 4), and emergency response (5–7)
can benefit from aerial vehicles that act as a flying human hand with extreme maneuverability, ma-
nipulation, and resiliency (MMR):(a) Maneuverability: to reach remote locations (8, 9), traverse
tight spaces (10–12), and inspect complex infrastructure, such as around and in-between pipes and
ducts (13,14) from any direction.(b) Manipulation: to push obstacles away (15), turn valves (16),
and press sensors against infrastructure to assess structural integrity (4).(c) Resiliency: to move
and apply forces with accuracy despite challenging disturbances, such as wind (17, 18), wall, ceil-
ing, and ground effects (19, 20), and counter-acting contact forces,e.g.,friction (21, 22).

Realizing MMR on aerial vehicles requires more than omnidirectional flight,i.e., flying in
arbitrary directions at any attitude; it also demands (I) vectoring of maximum thrust in any di-
rection, (II) almost-everywhere/global stability, and (III) compact and standard designs.Enabler
I: Vectoring of maximum thrust in any direction.Generating maximum thrust in any desired
direction —up to the capacity of the vehicle’s rotors and independently of vehicle orientation—
delivers maximum acceleration, contact force application, and disturbance rejection along any di-
rection.Enabler II: Almost-everywhere/global stability.Maintaining stability during motion
from any current position and orientation to any other guarantees reliable maneuverability and ma-
nipulation amid disturbances.Enabler III: Compact and standard designs.Building on compact
and standard platforms, such as quadrotors, the vehicle can access confined environments, inherit
technological maturity, and maintain a low-complexity structure.
No current aerial vehicle enjoys simultaneously Enablers I–III: structural and control limita-
tions either constrain the range of actuation, preventing Enablers I and/or II (1, 16, 23–37), or
require non-compact designs of vehicle-of-vehicles (38–40) or hexarotor/octarotor designs (2, 3,
41, 42), preventing Enabler III. We next review the state of the art in more detail, from fixed-tilt to
variable-tilt designs, and summarize the most relevant platforms in Table 1.

Fixed-tilt multirotors.Fixed-tilt multirotors lack Enabler I and cannot simultaneously realize
Enablers II and III. Fixed-tilt multirotors lack rotor articulation: each rotor generates thrust in a
static direction with respect to the vehicle body.(a) Fixed-tilt multirotors with all rotors point-
ing in the same direction, such as standard quadrotors, achieve Enabler III but not Enablers I and
II. The rotors collectively generate maximum thrust only in a single direction (46–50), falling short
of Enabler I. This also causes the inability to translate or apply force in lateral directions without
rotating, falling short of Enabler II.(b) Fixed-tilt multirotors with rotors pointing in different
directionsachieve Enabler II but not Enablers I and III. Omnidirectionality on this configuration
requires a large number of rotors oriented in fixed, different directions on the vehicle body (51–54):
seven or more unidirectional rotors (23, 25, 26), or six or more bidirectional rotors (1, 24, 27, 28),
all in optimized placement. Such designs impose complex mechatronic hardware and boxy struc-
tures (23,25,26), falling short of Enabler III. In addition, they always have rotors generating coun-
teracting thrust during operation, falling short of Enabler I.
Variable-tilt multirotors.Existing variable-tilt multirotors lack Enabler I due to the limited
ranges of rotor articulation (2, 3, 30–34, 36, 41, 42, 55–58) or thrust allocation constraints imposed
by the vehicle design (35); those that fulfill Enabler I cannot achieve Enabler III because of their
structural bulkiness (38–40).(a) Variable-tilt multirotors with limited rotor articulationsac-
rifice Enabler I and cannot simultaneously achieve Enablers II and III. The limited articulation, if
not mechanically bounding the maximum tilting angle (36,37,56), causes inter-rotor thrust cancel-
lation whenever the vehicle operates away from nominal hover, falling short of Enabler I. Designs
in this category realize the limited articulation through either synchronized or independent single-
axis rotor articulation. Synchronized rotor articulation (30, 36, 55–58) falls short of Enabler II
due to control singularity (30, 55) and mechanical constraints (36, 56). Independent single-axis
rotor articulation either empowers a quadrotor structure to achieve Enabler III but sacrifices En-
abler II (31–33) due to control singularities, or empowers a many-rotor vehicle to achieve Enabler
II (2, 3, 34, 41, 42) but sacrifices Enabler III, requiring complex mechatronic components that sup-

a morphable quadrotor design that promises Enablers I–III through co-designed control and thrust
allocation on two-axis articulated rotors; its hardware implementation, real-world deployment, and
resiliency under disturbances remained open.

Contributions
We present MorphQuad, a morphable multirotor that embodies Enablers I–III in a quadrotor struc-
ture, with fully-onboard localization and computation for (a) maneuverability, (b) manipulation,
and (c) resiliency (Movie 1).(a) Maneuverability:MorphQuad performs helical multi-revolution
motion while pointing the camera at a pipe—unprecedented on variable-tilt multirotors— for in-
spection, and pointing while hovering for hand tracking.(b) Manipulation:MorphQuad can
apply torque of4.92Nm and lateral force of4.55kg, comparable to a human wrist’s strength (59)
and sufficient for industrial applications (60), on top of hovering. We demonstrate these in valve
turning, wall perching and nail pressing, and whiteboard pushing.(c) Resiliency:MorphQuad
maintains accurate and stable hover at any attitudes under wind disturbances —even when the
wind is directed at a single rotor system. 1 Under impulsive and sustained physical displacements,
MorphQuad maintains an RMSE similar to free flight on the orthogonal axes to the disturbances.
Key to our approach is a co-design of hardware and control: on hardware, we independently
articulate each of the four rotor systems via two-axis gimbals for omnidirectional thrust vectoring;
on control, we introduce a thrust allocation that is energy-optimal, achieving maximum thrust
vectoring in any direction, and a generalized geometric controller that is almost-globally stable,
which we augment with disturbance adaptation; the thrust allocation allows inter-rotor cancellation
only to reduce downwash interference and avoid mechanical gimbal lock.

Results
First, we discuss our contributions on hardware and autonomy that embody Enablers I–III, and
then showcase the hardware experiment setup and evaluation results.
Hardware and autonomy
We present MorphQuad’s (A) hardware and (B) autonomy pipeline, as shown in Figure 2, co-
designed to realize Enablers I–III. Replacing the regular quadrotor’s fixed rotors with four inde-
pendently articulated rotor systems allows MorphQuad to continuously vector maximum thrust in
any direction (Enabler I). We deploy a provably-stable generalized geometric controller, paired
with an energy-optimal thrust allocation, to guarantee almost-everywhere stability for MorphQuad
(Enabler II). We integrate an off-the-shelf vision-based navigation architecture on a compact and
standard quadrotor structure with fully-onboard autonomy (Enabler III).
We first present the independently articulated rotor system that enables continuous omnidirec-
tional thrust vectoring, and then the autonomy pipeline that enables trajectory generation, local-
ization, and motion control for the fully-onboard experiments. Additional details on mechatronic
component selection and control synthesis are given in Materials and Methods.
Independently articulated rotor systems
We enable the continuous omnidirectional thrust vectoring on MorphQuad by mounting each rotor
system on a gimbal mechanism (Figure 3(A)) that is actuated across two perpendicular articulation
axes. The gimbals can continuously vector the rotor thrusts into any desired direction inside a
sphere. Each rotor system, in particular, is composed of two counter-rotating motors with the same
speed, and appropriate propellers that together generate thrust in the same direction. This design
choice eliminates gyroscopic precession of the rotating motor masses and the aerodynamic drag
effects from the propellers. Next, we elaborate on the gimbal design and rotor system.

Gimbals.The gimbal mechanism enables each rotor system to rotate up to±1080 ◦ along two
perpendicular axes (Figure 3(A)), directing the rotor thrust on the sphere through three full revo-
lutions from neutral (Figure 3(C)). This range is possible because we route the power and signal
wires through an opening at the center of the outer servo rotation axis, and enable the resulting
twist through slack along the wire length (Figure 3(B)). A belt-pulley transmission offsets the
outer servo from the rotation axis, leaving the center clear for the pass-through; the inner servo sits
inline with its axis and requires no such offset.2
Rotor systems.At the intersection of the two rotation axes of each gimbal, we mount a coaxial
motor pair to provide thrust. Each propeller rotates in the opposite direction at equal speeds (Fig-
ure 3(A)). The counter-rotation benefits the thrust vectoring and controller design by eliminating
two adverse effects that arise from a singular rotating propeller: (i) gyroscopic effect: a spinning
rotor produces a precession torque that resists gimbal motion; and (ii) aerodynamic drag: a singu-
lar rotating propeller receives a reaction torque from the air along the spin axis that is inseparable
from thrust generation. Without counter-rotation, the servo motors would need to overcome the
dynamic load from (i) during thrust vectoring, and each rotor’s thrust would couple with a torque
from (ii). Furthermore, the aerodynamic drag would complicate MorphQuad’s control and thrust
allocation design, in a similar way to (35), preventing Enabler I. The dual-motor solution elimi-
nates both the gyroscopic effect and the aerodynamic drag, leading to rapid and consistent servo
motion (Figure 3(D)) and low-complexity thrust allocation.
Autonomy pipeline
We introduce a fully-onboard autonomy pipeline for MorphQuad, outlined in Figure 2(B). Co-
designed with the hardware, the autonomy pipeline enables continuous omnidirectional vectoring
of maximum thrust, almost-everywhere stable motion and force control, and fully-onboard local-
ization without external motion capture or off-board computation. We inherit the standard PX4
quadrotor architecture and configure its perception, control, and thrust allocation modules for the
2Slip rings could be used instead to enable unlimited rotation along both axes.
13
PAGE 14
localization and navigation of MorphQuad. The autonomy pipeline consists of four stages that
translate the high-level setpoint commands into rotor and gimbal commands: (a) a trajectory plan-
ner that generates position and attitude trajectories based on the time-stamped setpoints we define
along a path for a task; (b) a generalized geometric controller that computes the force and torque
required for MorphQuad to track the planned trajectories; (c) an optimal thrust allocation that
realizes the required force and torque by calculating the rotor speed and gimbal angles of each
independently articulated rotor system; and (d) a state estimator that fuses camera and IMU mea-
surements to enable localization in GPS-denied environments, free of a motion capture system.
Trajectory planner.We implement a trajectory planner to convert user-defined position and
attitude setpoints into a smooth trajectory for MorphQuad to track since we design each task, in-
cluding manipulations, with a series of time-stamped setpoints. In contrast to planners for regular
quadrotors that consider coupled translation and rotation, our planner decouples them thanks to
MorphQuad’s ability to generate translational force at any orientation using independently articu-
lated rotor systems. We use quintic-polynomial trajectories that guarantee continuous accelerations
and smooth velocities (62) to connect the timed setpoints. We script the setpoints on the onboard
companion computer (Jetson) and send the converted trajectory snapshots to the flight controller
(Autopilot), in the form of reference states (Rd,ω d,p d,v d) at a frequency of100Hz.
Generalized geometric controller.Our generalized geometric controller computes the desired
force and torque for MorphQuad to track the reference states provided by the trajectory planner; it
leverages the omnidirectional thrust vectoring of MorphQuad to attract the vehicle to any arbitrary
desired state. In contrast to a geometric controller on regular quadrotors (63) that only computes
the thrust perpendicular to the body plane, we allow the geometric controller to independently
command a thrust force spanning all three translational axes, on top of a torque spanning all three
rotational axes. The generalization grants almost-everywhere exponential stability: MorphQuad
can track trajectories across independent translation inR 3, and at the same time, the rotation on
the entireSO(3)manifold with only zero-measure exceptions, without the orientation-dependent

translation seen on regular quadrotors. We augment the generalized geometric controller withL 1
adaptation (64); it allows MorphQuad to estimate and reject unmodeled disturbances from any
direction by vectoring thrust in the counteracting direction of the disturbance in real time. 3 We
derive the full generalized controller and adaptive law in the Materials and Methods section.
Thrust allocation.Our thrust allocation converts force and torque commands from the controller
into the angular velocities (Ωi) of the rotor systems, and the angular positions (αi,β i) of the gimbal
servos. The allocation drives MorphQuad’s four independently articulated rotor systems by solving
a linear inversion that respects mechanical gimbal-lock and permits inter-rotor thrust cancellations
to reduce downwash interference between rotors. We implement the allocation within the existing
PX4 mixer framework, preserving compatibility with standard multirotor toolchains. We derive the
full allocation in the Materials and Methods and prove its optimality in Supplementary Materials.
State estimation.Our state estimator provides state feedback of the vehicle’s current position
and orientation (R,ω,p,v) at800Hz by fusing visual-inertial odometry with inertial measure-
ments using an Extended Kalman Filter. The state feedback enables the generalized geometric
controller to compute the desired force and torque for trajectory tracking. We choose commer-
cially available sensors, a stereo camera for visual-inertial odometry, and an off-the-shelf IMU
suite, to maintain the compact standard design.

We evaluate MorphQuad’s co-designed hardware and autonomy in delivering maneuverability,
manipulation, and resiliency (MMR). We design three groups of experiments (Figure 1): (a) ma-

Maneuverability experiments.The pipe-inspection workspace contains two horizontal paral-
lel pipes (Figure 4(A)) with a vertical gap of1.3times MorphQuad’s side length, forcing tight
position tracking for collision-free traversal. The hand-tracking workspace has a human operator
standing1.5m in front of MorphQuad (Figure 4(B)); the operator extends one hand toward Mor-
phQuad’s onboard camera, and MorphQuad hovers in place, tracking the hand’s pointing direction
and matching its roll tilt in real time while the operator moves the hand.
Manipulation experiments.For the valve-turning task, we mount a valve on a wall3m above
the ground, with its30cm-long axis extruding outward at45 ◦ from the wall (Figure 5(A)); such
a valve would otherwise require a human operator on a ladder to operate. We equip MorphQuad
with a rigid “Y”-shaped passive end-effector at its front that enters the valve’s spokes to turn it.
For perching and nailing, we mount a styrofoam block on a wall2m above the ground; we attach
a nail perpendicularly to MorphQuad’s bottom via a neodymium magnetic connector that releases
cleanly after insertion (Figure 5(B)). For pushing, we place a wheeled whiteboard weighing30kg
unrestrained on the floor (Figure 5(C)); MorphQuad needs to perch on the whiteboard to push it.
Resiliency experiments.For wind disturbances, we direct a leaf blower at MorphQuad with its
nozzle within30cm of MorphQuad’s front face, producing an airflow of30m/s measured at the
same front face (Figure 6(A)). Unlike a wind tunnel (18) or fan array (61) that generates a uniform
freestream, this setup concentrates peak airflow at a small incidence area, producing spatially non-

uniform and time-varying force and torque disturbances that simultaneously load translation and
rotation —a regime distinct from, not easier than, a uniform freestream. For impulsive physical
displacements, a human operator wields a hammer to apply the physical displacement; the hammer
head makes direct contact with MorphQuad’s body, producing lateral pushes and off-centered picks
(Figure 6(B)). For sustained disturbances, the operator applies a pulling force via a tether connected
to MorphQuad through an inline force meter (Figure 6(C)).
Task implementation and evaluation metric.We define task-specific setpoints of each task
for the trajectory planner and record position and orientation tracking errors throughout. Because
MorphQuad achieves localization from onboard visual-inertial odometry, we report all tracking
errors in the world frame as estimated by the onboard state estimator. We do not evaluate the
accuracy of contact force and torque since neither the trajectory planner commands contact force
or torque, nor is the vehicle equipped with the necessary sensors. Specifically, for manipulation
tasks, MorphQuad relies on position and attitude references by the planner, and maintains accurate
trajectory tracking, upon treating any counteracting forces and torques as disturbances.
Evaluation summary. (a) Maneuverability: MorphQuad demonstrates simultaneous rotation
and translation, including720 ◦ continual rotation that is unprecedented on previous variable-tilt
multirotors. The trajectory tracking maintains a position RMSE below6cm and orientation RMSE
below5 ◦.(b) Manipulation: MorphQuad demonstrates omnidirectional force and torque appli-
cation, including a lateral force generation comparable to its takeoff mass (4.56kg) and its torque
application comparable to a human wrist (4.92Nm). Upon the completion of tasks, we report a
position RMSE below5cm and an attitude RMSE below3 ◦ along axes that are not affected by the
interaction.(c) Resiliency: MorphQuad maintains similar motion accuracy across different hover-
ing attitudes amid winds and push/pull/pick disturbances, even when a30m/s wind gust is directly
fed to one of its propellers. MorphQuad achieves a position RMSE of approximately8cm along
the dominant wind axis, and the orientation RMSE below5 ◦. MorphQuad maintains its position
RMSE2–4cm and orientation RMSE below5 ◦ on axes orthogonal to physical displacements.

Maneuverability: Simultaneous translation and multi-revolution rotation
We demonstrate MorphQuad’s motion stability and accuracy during omnidirectional flight, includ-
ing simultaneous translation and multi-revolution rotation, unprecedented on previous variable-tilt
multirotors. In particular, MorphQuad performs (a) pipe inspection via a continual720 ◦ pitch ro-
tation around a pipe while keeping the heading direction towards the pipe; it emulates practical
maintenance applications where the robot needs to traverse between pipes and keep onboard sen-
sors pointing towards a target and (b) real-time hand tracking using onboard vision while hovering;
it showcases MorphQuad’s quick and accurate response to dynamic and unpredictable target states.
Continual rotation for pipe inspection
MorphQuad executes the pipe-inspection task by simultaneously translating along a circle with a
1m diameter in a vertical plane around a horizontal pipe and pitching720 ◦ continually, as shown
in Figure 4(A). The narrow inter-pipe gap demands accurate position tracking for collision-free
operation despite the excessive rotation. MorphQuad achieves position errors below6cm RMSE
on all three axes and pitch errors at5.5 ◦ RMSE across the full trajectory. The demonstration of
simultaneous multi-revolution continual rotation during translation is unprecedented on previous
variable-tilt multirotors, demonstrating its maneuverability.
Hovering and pointing for hand tracking
Dynamic and unpredictable setpoints challenge the controller and hardware under conditions closer
to real-world pointing tasks than pre-scripted trajectories (Figure 4(B)). We implement an onboard
hand-orientation detector to estimate the operator’s hand pose from the front camera feed and send
orientation setpoints to the flight controller, so that MorphQuad follows the hand pose: pitch and
yaw authority allow the vehicle to point in any direction, effectively sweeping the camera over a
full sphere, while roll commands keep the hand upright in the camera frame.
Despite the dynamic hand movements, MorphQuad maintains a position RMSE of5.5cm dur-
ing hand tracking, confirming that the position controller remains decoupled from the orientation

for the higher orientation error relative to experiment (a).
Manipulation: Turning valves, perching and nailing, and pushing objects
We demonstrate MorphQuad’s capability of force and torque application —at a similar level to a
human wrist— in arbitrary directions. In particular, we design three tasks of distinct objectives
(Figure 5): (a) Turning a valve wheel; it requires torque application on top of motion control.
(b) Perching on a wall and pressing a nail into a surface; it requires sustained directional force ap-
plication on top of motion control. (c) Pushing a heavy wheeled object; it requires sustained force
application against an unrestrained moving surface, challenging the control resiliency. We remark
that all manipulation tasks remain open-loop with respect to contact: MorphQuad uses neither
force/torque sensors, nor visual servoing during manipulation; instead, the trajectory planner only
prescribes position and attitude setpoints. The vehicle follows the smooth trajectory that connects
the setpoints upon treating all counteracting contact forces and torques as disturbances.
Valve turning
MorphQuad approaches the valve at a45 ◦ pitch to align the end-effector with the spokes of the
valve wheel. Once engaged, MorphQuad locks its position and rotates about the valve axis to
complete the turning, as shown in Figure 5(A). As wheeled valves are common infrastructure
control mechanisms, this task highlights the potential of MorphQuad in maintenance automation.
During task execution, MorphQuad simultaneously holds position and rolls aligned with the
valve axis to generate a torque that rotates the wheel. The experiment is demanding since Mor-
phQuad operates close to the wall, on top of the unknown torque needed to turn the valve, the
aerodynamic wall effects perturb the airflow and risk the position control accuracy; consequently,
a high position error would cause collision between MorphQuad and the surrounding structures.

Despite the challenges, MorphQuad maintains position control throughout the valve turning
maneuver, with position RMSE at 4 cm along the z-axis and below 3 cm along the x- and y-axes.
Orientation RMSEs are3.9 ◦ in roll,7.7 ◦ in pitch, and2.4 ◦ in yaw. We attribute the elevated pitch

4.92Nm in pitch (and by symmetry in roll) during hover, confirming that the rotor system delivers
torque authority that is comparable to human wrists (59) and satisfies industrial specifications (60).
Perching and nailing
MorphQuad approaches the wall while continuously rotating its body, transitioning from nominal
hover to a wall-parallel pose; once the nail aligns with the target, the controller applies a sustained
force toward the wall to perch and press the nail into the styrofoam, as shown in Figure 5(B);
finally, MorphQuad moves up to break the magnetic connection and retreats from the wall. This
task demonstrates the application of a sustained force while holding an arbitrary pose that promises
MorphQuad in versatile manipulation.
MorphQuad maintains a tracking error similar to hover during the approach and rotation phase
(Figure 5(B)). Upon contact, noticeable oscillation appears as the nail resists insertion. We attribute
this oscillation to (i) compliance in the landing legs leads to vibration at impact with the wall, and
(ii) the close proximity of the propellers to the wall induces turbulence that disturbs the rotor thrust.
Despite these disturbances, the overall position RMSE remains approximately5cm across all three
axes, with orientation RMSEs of2.4 ◦,2.9 ◦, and2.3 ◦ in roll, pitch, and yaw, respectively.

Pushing objects
We task MorphQuad to push the whiteboard across the floor for around1m (Figure 5(C)), emu-

rigid wall, MorphQuad perches onto the whiteboard and drives it across the floor, requiring the con-

the interaction. Because the manipulation pipeline currently operates without feedback on contact
force, we feedforward a constant body-frame acceleration after perching to sustain the push.
We report, in Figure 5(C), a noticeably higher position error along the y-axis than in other

to the whiteboard surface. The controller maintains stable attitude tracking throughout the push,
with orientation RMSEs of approximately2.8 ◦ in roll,5.0 ◦ in pitch, and2.1 ◦ in yaw, confirming
that sustained contact does not compromise attitude stability.

Strong varying wind fields
We design four hover scenarios that span the range of real-world wind encounters: abrupt onset
from an off-axis direction, periodic sweeping across the front face of MorphQuad, abrupt onset
while the quadrotor hovers in a vertical (90 ◦ pitch) pose exposing its full lateral cross-section
(Figure 6(A)), and direct blowing into a single propeller creating highly asymmetric aerodynamic
interference. We sustain each scenario for 30 seconds while MorphQuad hovers at a fixed setpoint.
MorphQuad maintains stable hovering at different attitudes despite the winds. Because the
wind is predominantly oriented along the y-axis of MorphQuad, the largest positional displace-
ment appears in that direction with an RMSE of approximately 8 cm, while the x- and z-axes
remain closer to 4 cm. The controller maintains strong attitude tracking throughout, with orienta-
tion RMSEs of2.8 ◦ in roll,4.6 ◦ in pitch, and2.9 ◦ in yaw. These results validate the flight resiliency
of MorphQuad under unknown, strong, and time-varying aerodynamic disturbances.
Impulsive push and pick disturbances
We evaluate MorphQuad’s ability to recover from sudden displacements by applying disturbances
at irregular intervals. We apply two types of disturbances: lateral pushes to the quadrotor body
that induce translational displacements, and off-centered vertical pushes from below (picks) that
simultaneously displace MorphQuad and impart a rotational disturbance. Physical collisions are
an unavoidable reality in cluttered environments and during contact-based tasks.
Push disturbances along the y-axis displace the quadrotor by as much as 50 cm from its setpoint
(Figure 6(B)). Despite these large translational excursions, the controller holds the orthogonal axes
with errors comparable to maneuver tasks (x-axis RMSE: 2 cm; z-axis: 4 cm). Similarly, although
pitch reaches over20 ◦ of disturbance in both directions, the controller maintains roll and yaw
at4.3 ◦ and2.5 ◦ RMSE, respectively. The time-series data confirm decoupled recovery: large
positional displacements do not propagate into rotational errors, and large rotational disturbances
do not degrade translational tracking.

Sustained pull disturbances
We quantify MorphQuad’s maximum disturbance rejection capability by subjecting it to sustained
pulling forces. The operator gradually increases the tension until MorphQuad reaches its maxi-
mum sustainable resistance. The force sensor records a peak load of 4.55 kg, while the disturbance
force estimated from the position error and controller state reaches 3.56 kg. The 0.99 kg discrep-
ancy between the two measurements is attributed to online disturbance rejection that counteracts a
portion of the pulling force before it manifests as position error.
Consistent with the push disturbance results, a large y-axis displacement exceeding 80 cm does
not propagate into the orthogonal axes (Figure 6(C)): the x-axis RMSE remains at 4.4 cm and the
z-axis at 2 cm, while the controller holds roll and yaw at2.4 ◦ and1.0 ◦, respectively. The pitch
RMSE of5.8 ◦ reflects the slight upward angle of the tether pull.
We contextualize this result through a scatter plot in Figure 6(C) comparing the rotational er-
ror induced by translational displacement for MorphQuad against a simulated standard quadrotor
under identical tether loading conditions. For MorphQuad, increasing positional displacement pro-
duces virtually no growth in rotation error, confirming decoupled disturbance rejection. The sim-
ulated conventional quadrotor exhibits rapidly growing rotation error with displacement because
it must pitch or roll to generate the lateral thrust needed to counteract the positional disturbance.
This comparison quantitatively illustrates a fundamental advantage of MorphQuad’s independently
articulated rotor architecture: MorphQuad rejects external forces through independent thrust vec-
toring, whereas a standard quadrotor suffers coupled translational and rotational responses.

Discussion
Summary of results.MorphQuad is the first omnidirectional multirotor aerial vehicle to simul-
taneously achieve Enablers I–III. We validate MorphQuad, with fully-onboard autonomy, in MMR
experiments.(a) Maneuverability:MorphQuad rotates continuously beyond360 ◦ while trans-
lating around pipes, and points while hovering to track a hand.(b) Manipulation:MorphQuad
generates torque of4.92Nm and lateral force of4.55kg on top of hovering —comparable to a hu-
man wrist (59) and sufficient for industrial tasks (60)— enabling it to turn an inclined valve, perch
on a wall to press a nail, and push heavy objects.(c) Resiliency:MorphQuad holds against30m/s
wind from any direction —including wind directed into a single rotor system— impulsive pushes,
and sustained pulls. Across all tasks, MorphQuad holds position within2–5cm and orientation
within3–6 ◦ along undisturbed axes.
Significance.MorphQuad promises to enable autonomous infrastructure maintenance, contact-
based inspection, and emergency response with a single aerial platform.Infrastructure inspection
and maintenance: The pipe inspection demonstrates the simultaneous translation and beyond-
360◦ rotation needed for practical infrastructure inspection. The valve turning demonstrates the
human-level torque needed to operate industrial mechanisms at hard-to-reach sites as both work-
flows today rely on scaffolding, rope access, or a human operator on a ladder.Contact-based
inspection: The perching-and-nailing demonstrates the sustained directional human-level force
needed to press sensors against infrastructure surfaces for integrity assessment, regardless of sur-
face orientation.Emergency response: The object pushing, alongside resilience to wind, im-
pulsive pushes, and sustained pulls, demonstrates the obstacle-clearing capacity comparable to a
human wrist; it also demonstrates disturbance robustness needed in cluttered, wind-exposed, and
collapsed environments. All results are obtained with fully on-board sensing and computation —
no motion capture, no GPS, no off-board compute— removing the constraint that has confined
prior omnidirectional multirotor demonstrations to laboratory settings, and aligning MorphQuad
with the unstructured environments these applications demand.

Materials and Methods
We present the mechatronic components, dynamics, and control framework that enable Mor-
phQuad to realize vectoring of maximum thrust in any direction and almost-global stability on
a compact quadrotor design with fully onboard autonomy. We first identify the mechatronic com-
ponents of the vehicle structure, actuation, and onboard computing, then derive the translational
and rotational dynamics of MorphQuad, and finally present the three-layer control architecture:
a geometric controller for singularity-free trajectory tracking onSE(3), anL 1 adaptive augmen-
tation for disturbance compensation, and a null-space-aware thrust allocation that resolves inter-
rotor aerodynamic interference and gimbal lock. Together, these components translate high-level
SE(3)trajectory commands into individual rotor speeds and gimbal angles that demonstrate Mor-
phQuad’s maneuverability, manipulation, and resiliency.
Mechatronic design
Main body.We preserve the compact footprint of a conventional quadrotor by arranging the
four rotor systems in an H-configuration on a main body composed of two parallel carbon-fiber-
infused PLA plates (Figure 2(A)). The dual-plate design serves a structural and organizational role:
the ESCs and power distribution electronics sit protected between the plates, the top plate houses
the flight and task computers, and the bottom plate mounts the camera for visual-inertial odometry.
This arrangement retains the geometric profile needed to access confined and narrow environments
while housing the full actuation, sensing, and computation required for omnidirectional flight. The
takeoff mass, including batteries, is4.56kg, and all electronic and mechanical components are
commercially available off-the-shelf products.
Rotor systems.Each rotor system pairs two coaxial counter-rotating rotors with a two-axis gim-
bal (Figure 3(A)): the counter-rotating pair cancels rotor-induced drag torque and gyroscopic pre-
cession, and the gimbal orients the pair to direct thrust in any direction. Each gimbal axis is driven
by a DYNAMIXEL servo selected for its compact form factor and position feedback —XL330-
29
PAGE 30
M288-T on the inner axis, XC330-M288-T on the outer— and the eight servos daisy-chain over
a single UART bus to the flight computer. Each coaxial pair consists of two brushless T-Motor
VELOX 3008 1500KV motors driving 6-inch triple-blade propellers, sized for a rotor-pair thrust
of2.8kg that lets MorphQuad hover at40% throttle. Peak motor draw exceeds43.6A during fast
maneuvers, which sets our choice of independent ESCs rated at51A continuous current.
Computing and simulation.The onboard computing stack enables fully-onboard autonomy
without off-board compute or motion capture: a Pixhawk Autopilot FMUv6x flight controller runs
attitude and position control, an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano runs trajectory generation, and an Intel
RealSense stereo-vision depth camera supplies the VIO sensor stream (72,73). To validate the full
software stack prior to hardware deployment, we integrate MorphQuad’s structural, kinematic, and
dynamic models into a ROS and Gazebo software-in-the-loop (SITL) simulator.

Enhancement for inter-rotor downwash and gimbal lock
We enhance the minimum-norm thrust allocation (15) to account for (a) inter-rotor downwash
interference and (b) gimbal lock. In more detail:(a) Inter-rotor aerodynamic interference.
When the desired torqueτ=0, the thrust allocation (15) yields co-linear thrust vectorst i ∥f
for alli. If the desired force is alongi B, the outer servo positions becomeα i =±π/2with
βi = 0, so all four rotor systems point their downwash toward the opposing pair. This mutual
interference invalidates the thrust model in (2) and reduces the effective thrust of the affected
rotors.(b) Gimbal lock.If the desired force is alongj B withτ=0, the inner servo reaches
βi =±π/2, at which pointcosβ i = 0and the outer servoα i loses authority over the thrust
direction. To illustrate the practical consequence, consider MorphQuad hovering at aπ/2roll
angle so thatf= [0mg0] ⊤. If the controller subsequently commands a small perturbation

±π/2, saturating the servos and destabilizing flight. This singularity shares the same mathematical
root as mapping elements ofS 2 toT 2.
We exploit the overactuation of MorphQuad to enable both enhancements through the null
space ofM. BecauseM∈R 6×12 is a wide matrix, the linear systemw=M tis underdeter-
